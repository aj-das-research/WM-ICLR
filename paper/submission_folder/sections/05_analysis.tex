\paragraph{How does \ours{} compose a forecast?} Because \cref{eq:output} is additive, each forecast splits exactly into
the kept term $(1-g)\Z_0$, the moved term $g\mathbf{T}$ and the correction $\mathbf{r}$ (\cref{fig:method}, bottom): the
moving arm is carried by transport and the correction concentrates on newly revealed content. Copying measured features
also keeps the forecast sharp: at $k{=}10$ it retains 80\% of the true spatial contrast, 4\% more than Direct (77\%)
and 8\% more than AR (74\%; \cref{fig:mechanism}).

\paragraph{Causal role of transport.} We intervene on the trained model (\cref{fig:qualitative}). Setting the gate to
zero at test time, so that each forecast is $\Z_0+\mathbf{r}$, raises the error by \good{\koMoving\%} on moving patches and
by \koHighGate\% on patches with a high learned gate, but only by \koStatic\% on static patches: the transport pathway, not
the correction, carries the prediction of moving content. Replacing the future actions with those of another episode
changes the transport field \good{\steerRatio$\times$} more on moving than on static patches, so the actions steer
\emph{where} features are moved. Across all ten deciles of true motion, \ours{} has lower error than Direct
(\good{+\decileMin\% to +\decileMax\%}), and the win maps show the gains concentrated on the arm and gripper.

\paragraph{What does the transport field learn? (Q4)} Converted into an expected source displacement per patch, the
field concentrates on the moving arm, gripper and instruments, while the background keeps the identity path
(\cref{fig:qualitative}); \cref{fig:flow} compares it with RAFT optical flow \citep{teed2020raft}.

\paragraph{Where do the gains come from?} We split every test window into \emph{moving} patches (the 25\% with the
largest true feature change, a property of the data shared by all methods) and \emph{static} patches
(\cref{tab:regions}, \cref{app:analysis}). Two effects add up. On moving patches, transport predicts where content goes:
\ours{} has \good{\droidMovingVsDirect\%} lower error than Direct and \good{\droidMovingVsAR\%} lower than AR. On static
patches, every other learned predictor is \emph{worse than copying}, since regenerating the grid corrupts background
that should not change; \ours{}'s identity path keeps it intact, and it is the only learned model below persistence
(\good{\droidStaticVsPers\%} lower; \droidStaticVsAR\% lower than AR).

\paragraph{Does the moving arm land in the right place?} Read out as a segmentation and compared with SAM~2.1 masks of the
true future (\cref{app:segments}), \ours{} places the moving DROID arm \good{\segDroidPlaceRedAR{}\,px} closer than AR and
marginally closer than Direct (\segDroidPlaceRedDi{}\,px, CI \segDroidPlaceRedDiCI{}); on Hamlyn all learned models are within a pixel.

\paragraph{Is the future a transport? A model-free check of Proposition~\ref{prop:bayes}.} On moving patches of
\geoWindows{} DROID test windows, the single observed feature in the transport window closest to the true future (an
\emph{oracle move}) cuts the error of staying put by \geoOracleRed\% at $k{=}10$: half of the future is
already in the observed grid, displaced. \ours{} recovers \good{\geoShareShift\%} of this gain (Direct \geoShareDirect\%,
AR \geoShareAR\%), is better on \good{\geoWinDirect\%} of moving patches, and has lower error than Direct and AR in
\good{all 100} horizon-by-motion cells (\cref{fig:geometry,fig:anatomy}).
