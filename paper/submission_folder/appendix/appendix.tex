\section{Background and problem setting}\label{app:background}
\subsection{What the model sees and what it must predict}\label{app:setting}
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{setting.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The forecasting problem on real held-out episodes.} Three observed frames and the future actions are given; features at future steps are predicted. Right: true feature change over the $16{\times}16$ grid.}
  \label{fig:setting}
\end{figure}
\Cref{fig:setting} shows the task on two held-out episodes, one from DROID (a Franka arm in a real kitchen) and one from
the Open-H Hamlyn surgical tasks (a dVRK instrument over a training phantom). The world model receives the last $H{=}3$
frames $t{-}2,t{-}1,t$, encoded by a frozen DINOv2 encoder into $16{\times}16$ grids of 384-D patch features, the actions
applied between them, and a sequence of $K{=}10$ future action blocks (the commanded end-effector trajectory, bottom rows).
It must predict the feature grids $\Z_{t+1},\dots,\Z_{t+10}$, 3.3\,s into the future, without seeing any future frame.
The episodes are chosen by a fixed rule (largest feature change among test episodes; \cref{app:qualitative}).

The rightmost panels show where the features actually change between $t$ and $t{+}10$. Change is concentrated on the
robot and on the objects it touches: in these two windows a quarter of the patches carry more than 40\% of the total
change, while large parts of the scene change little. This is the structure that \ours{} exploits: most future
patch features are already present in the observed grid, displaced by motion, and only a small part is new content.

\subsection{Related formulations}\label{app:related-formulations}
Latent world models such as DINO-WM \citep{zhou2025dinowm}, V-JEPA 2-AC \citep{assran2025vjepa2} and LeWM
\citep{maes2026lewm} predict future features of a frozen or jointly trained encoder with a network whose output
\emph{replaces} the feature grid: absolute regression, usually rolled out one step at a time. Residual variants predict
$\Z_k-\Z_0$, which keeps static patches cheap but still requires the network to write the full displacement of
every moving patch (Proposition~\ref{prop:residual}). Video prediction in pixel space has long used explicit motion
(flow warping, dynamic filters, kernel-based transformations); \ours{} brings this structure to the feature space of
foundation models, where features are high-dimensional and semantically stable under motion, so that copying them is
accurate. Unlike optical-flow pipelines, the transport is never supervised with flow: it is learned end-to-end from the
feature forecasting loss, conditioned on future actions, and is used at every horizon directly from the observed grid.

\section{Extended method}\label{app:method}
\subsection{Architecture}\label{app:impl}
\paragraph{Architecture.} Width $d{=}384$, 6 heads, MLP ratio 4, memory encoder $L_e{=}4$ layers of
full self-attention over the $H\cdot N{=}768$ tokens of $H{=}3$ observed $16{\times}16$ grids. Query
decoder: $L_d{=}4$ layers, each with self-attention over the $N{=}256$ query tokens, cross-attention
to memory and an MLP, modulated by AdaLN-zero on $\mathbf{c}_k$. Action embedding: a 2-layer MLP
followed by a single-layer unidirectional GRU of width $d$. Transport: key dimension 64, window
$w{=}7$ over $S{=}3$ source frames (147 candidates per query), learned identity bias initialised at
$\beta{=}4$, and gate bias $-3$. Correction head: LayerNorm, then a zero-initialised linear map to $C$,
then a per-channel scale initialised at 1. Parameters: 21.75M (\ours{}) and 21.70M (Direct, AR, AR-TF).

\subsection{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:bayes}.} The conditional mean minimises expected squared error. Since $\Z_0$
is part of the context and $\E[\bm{\varepsilon}_k\mid\mathbf{P}_k,\Z_0,\mathbf{a}]=\mathbf{0}$,
$\E[\Z_k\mid c]=\E[\mathbf{P}_k\mid c]\,\Z_0+\E\big[\E[\bm{\varepsilon}_k\mid\mathbf{P}_k,c]\mid c\big]=\bar{\mathbf{P}}_k\Z_0$.
Each row of $\mathbf{P}_k$ is a one-hot vector, so each row of $\bar{\mathbf{P}}_k$ is a probability vector supported
on the same indices, hence on $\mathcal{W}(i)$ for row $i$. For $g=1$ and $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}$, \cref{eq:output}
gives $\hat{\Z}_{k,i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{W}(i)}\pi_{k,i}(j)\Z_{0,j}$. Softmax weights with logits
$\ell_j=\log\bar{P}_{k,ij}$ on the support and $\ell_j\to-\infty$ elsewhere converge to row $i$ of $\bar{\mathbf{P}}_k$,
so $\hat{\Z}_k\to\bar{\mathbf{P}}_k\Z_0$. With $S>1$ source frames the same argument applies to a motion model whose
rows select patches of any observed frame. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:residual}.} An additive head outputs $\Z_0+\mathbf{R}$; matching
$\mathbf{P}\Z_0$ requires $\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{I})\Z_0$, whose $i$-th row is
$\mathbf{z}_{0,\pi(i)}-\mathbf{z}_{0,i}$; summing squared row norms gives the claim. For \ours{}, the one-hot
transport onto $\pi(i)$ (the limit above) with $g=1$ reproduces $\mathbf{P}\Z_0$ with $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}$. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:compound}.} Let $e_j=\|\hat{\Z}_j-\Z_j\|$ for the recursive rollout
$\hat{\Z}_j=f(\hat{\Z}_{j-1})$ and $\Z_j=f(\Z_{j-1})+\bm{\eta}_j$ with $\|\bm{\eta}_j\|\le\delta$. Then
$e_j\le\|f(\hat{\Z}_{j-1})-f(\Z_{j-1})\|+\|\bm{\eta}_j\|\le Le_{j-1}+\delta$ with $e_0=0$, so
$e_k\le\delta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}L^j$. A direct predictor computes $\hat{\Z}_k$ from observed inputs only, so its error at
horizon $k$ does not depend on its predictions at other horizons. $\square$

\subsection{Plug-in heads for published world models}\label{app:plugin}
For a published backbone with prediction $\mathbf{P}_k$, the head outputs $\hat\Z_k=(1-g_k)\mathbf{P}_k+g_k\mathbf{T}_k$,
where $\mathbf{T}_k$ is the local $7{\times}7$ soft transport (with identity bias) of \emph{observed} encoder tokens, and
the gate $g_k=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\h+b)$ has $\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}$, $b=-4$ at initialisation, so training starts
numerically from the unmodified backbone. Nothing else in the backbone, loss or schedule changes.
\textbf{V-JEPA 2-AC.} Queries come from the predictor's normalised hidden state for each output block (1024-D), keys and
values from the layer-normalised ViT-g/16 tokens ($16{\times}16$, 1408-D) of the last $S{=}3$ measured frames, with a
learned per-source key offset (0.16M parameters). The fine-tuning objective is the released one (teacher-forced L1 plus
rollout L1). During rollouts the predictor consumes $\hat\Z_k$ as its own prediction, while $\mathbf{T}_k$ only ever
reads measured tokens.
\textbf{DINO-WM.} We subclass the official \texttt{ViTPredictor} (commit \texttt{0a9492f}, otherwise unmodified): queries
come from the predictor's output token, keys and values from its input token at the same slot, on the $14{\times}14$
DINOv2 patch grid ($S{=}1$); proprioceptive and action channels are left untouched. The extra parameters are drawn from a
private random generator, so data order, initialisation and dropout of the base predictor are identical in both arms.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{vjepa_plugin.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{V-JEPA 2-AC with and without the \ours{} head (DROID).} (a) Validation error while fine-tuning (2 seeds). (b) Mean gate. (c) Test skill per horizon.}
  \label{fig:vjepa-plugin}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Plug-in dynamics.} \Cref{fig:vjepa-plugin} shows both fine-tuning arms over their full 3{,}000-step budget.
With the head, validation error is lower at every evaluation step for both seeds. The gate starts at $0.018$, so the
model begins as the released predictor, and opens to about $0.55$ within the first 250 steps, rising to about $0.65$: the
backbone learns to route most of its forecast through transport of measured tokens. Both arms reach their best
validation error early and then slowly overfit our training split; the test numbers use the best validation checkpoint
of each run. The skill gain holds at every horizon and grows from $+11.5$ points at $k{=}1$ to $+13.1$ at $k{=}10$.

\section{Experimental details}\label{app:exp}
\subsection{Datasets and splits}\label{app:data}
\input{submission_folder/tables/datasets.tex}
\paragraph{DROID.} We use 24 official full-release shards (22.0\,GB) containing 1{,}126 successful
episodes. Splits are assigned by recording session with a salted hash, so no session appears in two
splits. Frames are subsampled every 5 native steps (15\,Hz $\to$ 3\,Hz), and each action block
concatenates the 5 commanded Cartesian poses and gripper positions ($5\times7{=}35$).
\paragraph{Open-H-Embodiment Hamlyn.} Seven dVRK training tasks, released under CC-BY-4.0 in LeRobot
format. We use the \texttt{observation.images.color} stream, which the release labels ``endoscope''
but which is an Intel RealSense scene camera ($848{\times}480$). One step is 1/3\,s: stride 10 at
30\,Hz, or stride 5 for the 15\,Hz tissue-lifting task. Each action block holds five commands at
15\,Hz, each an 8-D pose-and-gripper target for each arm ($5\times16{=}80$). Episodes shorter than 14
steps (6 of 1{,}019) are removed. Splits are 70/15/15 per task, assigned by a hash of the episode id.
\paragraph{IWS.} \pend{}
\paragraph{Planning suites.} PushT, TwoRoom and Reacher expert datasets from the LeWM release
(HDF5). Episodes are split disjointly into train and validation sets for world-model training.
Evaluation follows the official protocol (\cref{app:planning}).

\subsection{Baselines}\label{app:baselines}
\paragraph{Matched predictors.} All learned baselines share \ours{}'s memory encoder, decoder, width,
depth, optimiser and schedule, and differ only in the output head and rollout. This isolates the
contribution of transport from those of capacity and spatial communication. Direct has the same
cross-patch attention as \ours{} and differs only in lacking the transport and gate.
\paragraph{DINO-WM-style (AR-TF).} Following \citet{zhou2025dinowm}, the predictor regresses absolute
next-step patch features with teacher forcing and is rolled out autoregressively at test time. We
use the same frozen DINOv2 patches at native resolution.
\paragraph{External reference: V-JEPA 2-AC.} We evaluate the released V-JEPA 2-AC \citep{assran2025vjepa2}
(ViT-g encoder and 300M action-conditioned predictor, trained on DROID) zero-shot on exactly our test windows, with its own
preprocessing, action convention and autoregressive rollout. Because feature spaces differ, we compare the fraction of
persistence error each model removes in its own space (\cref{tab:external}); we also fine-tune it on our training split with
and without the \ours{} head under an identical budget. Its training data likely include our test trajectories.
\input{submission_folder/tables/external.tex}
\paragraph{Earlier variant.} Our earlier compact variant (4$\times$4 pooled grids, per-patch temporal
predictor, $\tanh$-bounded correction) is reported for continuity in \cref{app:ablations}.

\subsection{Training and compute}\label{app:training}
\paragraph{Training.} AdamW ($\beta{=}(0.9,0.95)$, weight decay 0.05), peak learning rate
$3{\times}10^{-4}$ with 800 warm-up steps and cosine decay, 16k steps, batch 48, bf16 autocast,
gradient clipping at 1.0. Validation MSE (averaged over horizons, windows at stride 2) is computed
every 2k steps, and the best checkpoint is used for testing. AR is trained through its full
10-step rollout. AR-TF is trained with teacher forcing and rolled out at test time. All features for
a split are kept in GPU memory as float16 and windows are gathered by index.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments ran on NVIDIA H200 GPUs, packing at most two learned predictors
per GPU. \pend{}

\subsection{Statistical procedures}\label{app:stats}
Each test episode contributes the mean of its windows (stride 2). For each method and seed we
average over episodes, and tables report the mean over three training seeds. For a paired
comparison between methods A and B, we compute per-episode differences (averaged over seeds) and
draw 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples of episodes to form a percentile 95\% interval. For DROID, we
resample sessions instead of episodes to respect the session structure. Primary contrasts (\ours{}
against each baseline on each dataset) are Holm-adjusted within each dataset.

\subsection{Planning results and faithfulness of the harness}\label{app:planning-results}
\paragraph{Faithfulness check.} With the released LeWM PushT checkpoint and the official protocol,
our harness reaches 45/50, 44/50 and 43/50 successes for planner seeds 42/43/44 (mean 88\%). An
adapter check confirms that our pluggable predictor interface produces exactly the native LeWM
planning costs (maximum absolute difference 0). The LeWM paper reports $96\pm2.8$, averaged over
training seeds, whereas the released checkpoint is a single training seed. On TwoRoom and Reacher the
released checkpoints reach 43/41/44 and 42/44/41 successes (85.3\% and 84.7\%), against 87 and 86
reported by \citet{maes2026lewm}. Planning takes 0.41\,s (PushT) and about 1.5\,s (TwoRoom, Reacher)
per CEM plan on one H200.

\paragraph{Accuracy versus action sensitivity.} A forecast that stays close to $\Z_0$ can score
well on average while barely responding to the commanded actions, and a planner then cannot rank
candidate action sequences. We measure this directly as the rate at which true future actions yield
lower error than actions from another episode (\cref{fig:tradeoff}, left). Anchored predictors can reach low
open-loop error while reacting weakly to actions; the action-contrastive objective moves \ours{} toward high
accuracy \emph{and} high sensitivity. \pend{}

\paragraph{Does the moving arm land in the right place?} Feature error does not show \emph{where} a forecast puts the
arm, so we read a segmentation out of every forecast (\cref{fig:segments}). SAM~2.1 \citep{ravi2025sam2} tracks the robot
arm (the instruments on Hamlyn) through the true future frames, and each forecast patch is labelled arm or background by
nearest-neighbour transfer from the observed frame alone (protocol below). \emph{Placement} is the distance between the
centroid of the cells a forecast labels as arm and the centroid of the true mask at $t{+}k$. On the half of the
\segDroidWindows{} held-out DROID windows where the arm moves most, the mean placement error over $k$ is
\segDroidPlaceSW{}\,px for \ours{}, \segDroidPlaceDi{} for Direct, \segDroidPlaceAR{} for AR and \segDroidPlacePers{} for
persistence (\segDroidPlaceFrame{} frame): \ours{} removes \good{\segDroidPlaceRedAR{}\,px} relative to AR (95\% CI
\segDroidPlaceRedARCI{}), but only \segDroidPlaceRedDi{}\,px relative to Direct (\segDroidPlaceRedDiCI{}), a marginal
difference; at $k{=}10$ \ours{} and Direct tie (\segDroidPlaceSWLast{} vs.\ \segDroidPlaceDiLast{}\,px). On Hamlyn all
learned models place the instruments within a pixel of each other (\ours{} \segHamlynPlaceSW{}, Direct
\segHamlynPlaceDi{}, AR \segHamlynPlaceAR{}\,px; paired reductions \segHamlynPlaceRedDi{} \segHamlynPlaceRedDiCI{} and
\segHamlynPlaceRedAR{} \segHamlynPlaceRedARCI{}\,px), all far ahead of persistence (\segHamlynPlacePers{}\,px). Overlap
tells the same story: on moving DROID windows the arm read out of \ours{} has IoU \good{\segDroidMovGainDi{}} points above
Direct (\segDroidMovGainDiCI{}) and \good{\segDroidMovGainAR{}} above AR (\segDroidMovGainARCI{}); on Hamlyn it ties
Direct (\segHamlynMovGainDi{}, \segHamlynMovGainDiCI{}; \cref{tab:segments}). The examples in \cref{fig:segments}a are
selected for a large \ours{} placement advantage and illustrate what that advantage looks like; the claims rest on the
averages.

\paragraph{Protocol.} We ask whether the moving part of the scene lands where it should in each forecast, without
decoding pixels. \emph{Reference masks.} On the observed frame $t$ (the last history frame) of every held-out window
(test split; the planning suites have no test split, so their validation split is used; stride 2 over all episodes, and an evenly spaced subset of 1{,}500 windows where there are more),
Grounding DINO \citep{liu2024groundingdino} detects a fixed per-dataset text prompt (DROID, Bridge, RT-1: ``a robot arm'';
Hamlyn: ``a surgical instrument'', up to two non-overlapping boxes; IWS: the manipulated T block, box or rope). Its
top box prompts SAM~2.1 (Hiera-L) \citep{ravi2025sam2}, which segments frame $t$ and \emph{tracks} the mask through the
true future frames $t{+}1,\dots,t{+}K$ with its video memory. Frames are segmented at their native aspect ratio; each
mask keeps its largest connected component and any component with $\ge$25\% of its area. For the rendered planning
suites a text prompt is unreliable, so an exact colour threshold of the agent/object is used instead. \emph{Quality
control.} A window is kept only if the detection score is $\ge 0.3$, the mask covers 3--128 of the 256 patches at $t$,
the track is never lost and the mask area never changes by more than $3\times$ between consecutive frames; on DROID
this keeps \segDroidWindows{} of \segDroidConsidered{} windows (rejected: \segDroidRejNoDetection{} without a
confident detection, \segDroidRejFgAreaAtT{} with an arm too small or too large at $t$, \segDroidRejLostTrack{} lost
tracks, \segDroidRejAreaJump{} area jumps), and on Hamlyn \segHamlynWindows{} of \segHamlynConsidered{}.
\emph{Reading a mask out of a forecast.} Masks are area-averaged onto the $16{\times}16$ patch grid (a patch is
foreground if $\ge$50\% covered). Each patch of a forecast $\hat\Z_k$ is labelled by nearest-neighbour transfer
from the observed frame only: it is foreground if its most similar (cosine) foreground patch of $\Z_0$ is more similar
than its most similar background patch. This labeller was fixed in a DROID pilot as the one with the highest IoU when
applied to the \emph{true} future features, a criterion that involves no forecast (on DROID at $k{=}10$:
nearest-neighbour \segDroidOracleNn{}, $k$-means prototypes \segDroidOracleMulti{}, single prototypes
\segDroidOracleProto{}). \emph{Metric.} IoU between the forecast mask and the tracked SAM mask of the true future frame,
per horizon and averaged over $k$, on all windows and on \emph{moving} windows (overlap of the masks at $t$ and $t{+}K$
below the median). CIs are 95\% bootstrap intervals over episodes; gains are paired differences per episode.
\emph{Placement.} The predicted centroid is the mean of the centres of the cells labelled arm; the true centroid is
the coverage-weighted mean of cell centres under the tracked SAM mask; distances are in pixels of the displayed
native-aspect frame (DROID \segDroidPlaceFrame{}, Hamlyn \segHamlynPlaceFrame{}) and, in \texttt{summary.json}, in
patches. At a given $k$, windows in which any compared method labels no cell are dropped for all methods (paired; this
affects at most 1\% of DROID and 3\% of Hamlyn windows). \emph{Examples} show where \ours{} does best, by a fixed rule: among QC-passing moving
windows in which \ours{}'s $k{=}10$ placement error is below its median and the labeller applied to the \emph{true} future
features is at least median-accurate (a method-independent sanity check), the two (from distinct episodes) with the
largest $k{=}10$ placement advantage $\min(\text{Direct},\text{AR})-\text{\ours{}}$. All methods are always shown on the
same window and the same frame. The examples are not representative; averages and paired CIs are in
\cref{tab:placement,tab:segments}. Checkpoints are the final ones used in the main tables.
\paragraph{Results.} \Cref{tab:placement} lists placement error and \cref{tab:segments} IoU per dataset and method (\cref{fig:segments-droid,fig:segments-openh-hamlyn}
show both example windows per dataset). On DROID, \ours{} has the highest IoU at every reported horizon, on all and on
moving windows, with paired CIs that exclude zero against both Direct and AR. On Hamlyn the instruments move less
within ten steps and all three learned predictors are within half an IoU point: \ours{} ties Direct and is marginally
ahead of AR; the segmentation view does not separate the methods there. Datasets whose ShiftWM/Direct checkpoints are
not trained yet are marked pending and are filled in by re-running \texttt{scripts/v2/segments\_all.sh}.
\paragraph{Caveats.} (i) The readout measures where a forecast puts content that \emph{looks like} the observed
foreground; it cannot credit content that was not visible at $t$. (ii) The labeller is imperfect: applied to the true
future features it reaches IoU \segDroidOracleNn{} on DROID at $k{=}10$, so absolute values are lower bounds on
localisation quality; on Hamlyn several forecasts slightly exceed this reference, because smoother forecasts can be
labelled more easily than the true features. (iii) SAM~2.1 occasionally includes a grasped object or tool-attached
structure in the tracked mask; QC removes lost or exploding tracks but
not such merges. On Hamlyn most rejected windows (\segHamlynRejFgAreaAtT{} of \segHamlynRejected{}) are close-ups in which
the instruments cover more than half of the frame. (iv) One seed per method.
