ABSTRACT
Latent world models forecast in the feature space of a frozen visual foundation
model, but at every future step they regenerate the entire feature grid, even though
most of a manipulation scene only moves. We introduce SHIFTWM, a world
model that forecasts by moving what it has already seen. For each preﬁx of future
actions, SHIFTWM predicts a local soft transport of observed patch features, a
gate that decides where transport is used, and a correction for content that trans-
port cannot explain. We show that the optimal forecast under motion is itself
such a transport, that additive heads must instead learn the full feature contrast
along the motion, and that anchoring every horizon to measured features prevents
compounding error. On held-out real robot video (DROID), SHIFTWM removes
27.5% of persistence error against 17.4% for a matched recursive predictor, and
it is the best predictor on seven surgical robot tasks. SHIFTWM also works as a
plug-in head for published world models: added to V-JEPA 2-AC and ﬁne-tuned
under an identical budget, it lowers forecast error by 15.7% (24.6% →36.4%
skill); added to DINO-WM with its ofﬁcial code, it lowers latent prediction error
by 8.0% on PushT but raises it by 18.4% on Wall. The learned transport ﬁeld and
gate are explicit, inspectable tensors that show where the model predicts motion.

et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b; Sobal et al., 2025). These predictors are generative in feature space.
At every future step they output the complete grid of patch features, usually by feeding their own
predictions back as inputs. In manipulation and surgical video, however, most of the scene persists:
tissue, tools, the table and the robot move, but they are rarely created or destroyed. Regenerating
persistent content at every step wastes capacity and lets errors compound under recursion.
We ask a simple question. Can a latent world model forecast by transporting features it has already
observed, and synthesise only what transport cannot explain? Motion-based video prediction an-
swered a version of this question in pixel space (Finn et al., 2016; Ebert et al., 2017; De Brabandere
et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2017). The idea has not been tested for foundation-feature world models used
for planning, where features are semantic, high-dimensional and coarse in space. Three questions
are open: whether transport helps once the predictor is strong, whether it survives held-out real data,
and what it costs for control.
We introduce SHIFTWM (Figures 1 and 2). A memory encoder reads the patch features of the last
few observed frames. For each future step, a decoder conditioned on the causal action-preﬁx state
produces per-patch queries. Each query (i) attends to a local window of observed feature locations
and copies their content, (ii) gates between staying in place and moving, and (iii) adds a learned
correction. Every horizon is predicted directly from measured features, so errors do not compound
through recursion, and the transport weights form a motion ﬁeld that we can inspect and compare
against optical ﬂow.
What is new.
SHIFTWM changes what a latent world model predicts. Instead of regressing the
next feature grid, it predicts, for every future step, a motion of the grid it has already observed:
a per-patch transport over measured features, a gate and a residual. Three properties distinguish
it from prior work. (i) Unlike pixel-warping predictors (Finn et al., 2016; Ebert et al., 2017; Liu
et al., 2017), it moves semantic foundation features that planners consume directly, and the move
is conditioned on an explicit action preﬁx. (ii) Unlike recursive latent world models (Zhou et al.,
2025; Maes et al., 2026b; Assran et al., 2025), every horizon is computed directly from measured
features, so errors cannot compound through feedback. (iii) Its intermediate quantities—where each
patch comes from, whether it moves, and what is new—are explicit tensors that can be inspected
and measured (Figure 11).
Contributions.
• Method. To our knowledge, SHIFTWM is the ﬁrst latent world model that forecasts by action-
conditioned transport of frozen foundation features. It is a drop-in output head for any frozen
patch encoder and adds <0.3% parameters to a matched predictor.
• Theory. Under a motion model, the MSE-optimal forecast is a transport of observed features;
additive heads must learn the feature contrast along the motion; recursive rollouts compound
error (Section 3.1).
• Held-out evidence. Against predictors with the same backbone, SHIFTWM has the lowest held-
out error on real robot video (DROID; 7.6% lower error than Direct and 12.2% lower than re-
cursive AR) and on seven surgical robot tasks, and we evaluate it on IWS, BridgeData V2, RT-1,
Language-Table and three planning suites.
• Plug-in for published world models. Added to V-JEPA 2-AC, the head lowers forecast error by
15.7% under an identical ﬁne-tuning budget; added to DINO-WM with its ofﬁcial code, it lowers
latent prediction error by 8.0% on PushT, while on Wall it raises it by 18.4% (Section 4.3).
• Mechanism.
We show that transport keeps forecasts sharp where regression blurs, that the
learned ﬁeld follows measured motion, and how an action-contrastive objective keeps anchored
forecasts sensitive to actions for planning.

Generative world models and model-based reinforcement learning.
Pixel-space generative
world models learn high-ﬁdelity, action-controllable video simulators. NWM conditions a diffusion
transformer on navigation actions (Bar et al., 2025); Cosmos and Cosmos-Predict2.5 provide large
video foundation models for physical AI (NVIDIA, 2025a;b) that Cosmos Policy adapts for con-
trol (Kim et al., 2026); Ctrl-World, IRASim, UniSim and IWS target robot manipulation and policy
evaluation (Guo et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026); and the Genie
family generates interactive environments from video, image or text prompts (Bruce et al., 2024;
Parker-Holder & Fruchter, 2025). Model-based reinforcement learning instead learns task-speciﬁc
latent dynamics jointly with rewards and policies, from PlaNet and DreamerV3 to TD-MPC2, IRIS
and DIAMOND (Hafner et al., 2019; 2025; Hansen et al., 2024; Micheli et al., 2023; Alonso et al.,
2024). These systems optimize sample ﬁdelity or online return with their own encoders, data and,
often, billions of parameters, so they do not share our contract of a ﬁxed frozen encoder, identi-
cal observations and actions, and targets in common feature coordinates. We therefore treat them
as complementary rather than as compute-matched baselines: they address rendering ﬁdelity and
online control, whereas we isolate the output parameterization of a compact predictor on a ﬁxed
representation.
Transformation- and transport-based prediction.
Predicting the future by moving existing con-
tent has a long history in video prediction. CDNA and DNA predict action-conditioned convolu-
tional kernels or pixel-motion distributions that transform the previous frame (Finn et al., 2016),
dynamic ﬁlter networks generate input-dependent ﬁlters (De Brabandere et al., 2016), and Vondrick
and Torralba transform past pixels with adversarially trained transformers (Vondrick & Torralba,
2017). SNA adds temporal skip connections to the ﬁrst observed image to handle occlusion (Ebert
et al., 2017), while ﬂow-warping approaches synthesize frames by resampling along predicted voxel
ﬂow (Liu et al., 2017); SAVP and MCVD extend pixel prediction with stochastic and diffusion
models (Lee et al., 2018; Voleti et al., 2022). Recent robot world models reintroduce explicit mo-
tion: FlowDreamer predicts 3D scene ﬂow before diffusion rendering (Guo et al., 2026a), AHEAD
forecasts VLA patch tokens conditioned on optical-ﬂow velocity (Syed et al., 2026), and anchored
navigation world models resist drift through anchor frames and epipolar guidance (Luan et al., 2026).
Object-centric models such as SlotFormer and SOLD impose structure through slots (Wu et al., 2023;
Mosbach et al., 2025). ShiftWM differs from these designs in four respects. It transports frozen foun-
dation features rather than pixels; its source is a ﬁxed memory of observed feature grids that remains
unchanged at every horizon, so predicted states are never fed back as inputs; each horizon-h forecast
is produced directly from the action preﬁx a1:h; and the transport is a local softmax over a spatial
neighborhood, combined with a gated identity path and a learned correction for disocclusion and
appearance change.

Matched baselines.
To isolate the output head, all learned predictors share the same memory
encoder, decoder, width and depth. Direct replaces Equation (2) with Z0 + Wohk. It has the
same cross-patch attention, so it controls for spatial communication. AR predicts a residual to the
most recent grid and is rolled out recursively, with gradients through the rollout. AR-TF follows
DINO-WM: it is trained with teacher forcing to predict absolute features and is rolled out at test
time. Persistence copies Z0, and Linear extrapolates the last feature difference.
Planning.
For control we plug each predictor into the same model-predictive planner (CEM) with
the ofﬁcial budgets of each benchmark. The cost is the feature distance between the predicted grid
and the goal grid (Section 4.5).

(Section 5)? Selection uses validation splits only; test splits are scored once.

Models and metrics.
All learned predictors share a frozen DINOv2-S/14 encoder (16×16 patches,

HELD-OUT FORECASTING ACROSS ROBOTS AND SURGERY (Q1)
Table 1 compares SHIFTWM with predictors that share its backbone and differ only in how the
forecast is formed. On held-out DROID, SHIFTWM achieves the lowest error of all methods: it
removes 27.5% of the persistence error, against 21.6% for Direct and 17.4% for AR, i.e. 7.6% lower
error than Direct, 12.2% lower than the rollout-trained AR and 34.3% lower than the DINO-WM-
style AR-TF, whose teacher-forced absolute predictions drift below persistence once rolled out. The
same ordering holds on the seven surgical tasks of Open-H, where SHIFTWM is 3.6% below Direct
and 4.0% below AR. Figure 5 resolves the error by horizon: recursive predictors accumulate error
as they consume their own forecasts (Proposition 3), while SHIFTWM stays lowest at every step.

V-JEPA 2-AC.

ANALYSIS
How does SHIFTWM compose a forecast?
Because Equation (2) is additive, each forecast splits
exactly into the kept term (1 −g)Z0, the moved term gT and the correction r (Figure 2, bottom):
the moving arm is carried by transport and the correction concentrates on newly revealed content.
Copying measured features also keeps the forecast sharp: at k=10 it retains 80% of the true spatial
contrast, 4% more than Direct (77%) and 8% more than AR (74%; Figure 11).
Causal role of transport.
We intervene on the trained model (Figure 6). Setting the gate to zero at
test time, so that each forecast is Z0 +r, raises the error by 56.8% on moving patches and by 35.9%
on patches with a high learned gate, but only by 1.8% on static patches: the transport pathway, not
the correction, carries the prediction of moving content. Replacing the future actions with those
of another episode changes the transport ﬁeld 2.8× more on moving than on static patches, so the
actions steer where features are moved. Across all ten deciles of true motion, SHIFTWM has lower
error than Direct (+5.1% to +13.2%), and the win maps show the gains concentrated on the arm
and gripper.

patch, the ﬁeld concentrates on the moving arm, gripper and instruments, while the background

Where do the gains come from?
We split every test window into moving patches (the 25% with
the largest true feature change, a property of the data shared by all methods) and static patches
(Table 17, Appendix E.1). Two effects add up. On moving patches, transport predicts where content
goes: SHIFTWM has 7.2% lower error than Direct and 10.0% lower than AR. On static patches,
every other learned predictor is worse than copying, since regenerating the grid corrupts background
that should not change; SHIFTWM’s identity path keeps it intact, and it is the only learned model
below persistence (2.1% lower; 15.5% lower than AR).
Does the moving arm land in the right place?
Read out as a segmentation and compared with
SAM 2.1 masks of the true future (Appendix E.2), SHIFTWM places the moving DROID arm
+2.1 px closer than AR and marginally closer than Direct (+0.6 px, CI [+0.0, +1.1]); on Hamlyn
all learned models are within a pixel.
Is the future a transport? A model-free check of Proposition 1.
On moving patches of 3,923
DROID test windows, the single observed feature in the transport window closest to the true future
(an oracle move) cuts the error of staying put by 51% at k=10: half of the future is already in the
observed grid, displaced. SHIFTWM recovers 79% of this gain (Direct 71%, AR 69%), is better on
64% of moving patches, and has lower error than Direct and AR in all 100 horizon-by-motion cells
(Figures 12 and 13).

CONCLUSION
SHIFTWM forecasts by moving what it has already seen, plus a small correction. It is the most
accurate predictor on held-out robot and surgical video, improves V-JEPA 2-AC and DINO-WM on
PushT as a plug-in head (not DINO-WM on Wall), and makes predicted motion explicit. Limita-
tions. Pixel metrics rely on a separate decoder; newly revealed content depends on the correction;
the surgical data are scene-camera views of dVRK training tasks, not in-vivo endoscopy.

A.3
RELATED FORMULATIONS
Latent world models such as DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2025), V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025)
and LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b) predict future features of a frozen or jointly trained encoder with a
network whose output replaces the feature grid: absolute regression, usually rolled out one step at
a time. Residual variants predict Zk −Z0, which keeps static patches cheap but still requires the
network to write the full displacement of every moving patch (Proposition 2). Video prediction in
pixel space has long used explicit motion (ﬂow warping, dynamic ﬁlters, kernel-based transforma-
tions); SHIFTWM brings this structure to the feature space of foundation models, where features are
high-dimensional and semantically stable under motion, so that copying them is accurate. Unlike
optical-ﬂow pipelines, the transport is never supervised with ﬂow: it is learned end-to-end from the
feature forecasting loss, conditioned on future actions, and is used at every horizon directly from the
observed grid.
B
EXTENDED METHOD
B.1
ARCHITECTURE
Architecture.
Width d=384, 6 heads, MLP ratio 4, memory encoder Le=4 layers of full self-
attention over the H · N=768 tokens of H=3 observed 16×16 grids. Query decoder: Ld=4 layers,
each with self-attention over the N=256 query tokens, cross-attention to memory and an MLP,
modulated by AdaLN-zero on ck. Action embedding: a 2-layer MLP followed by a single-layer
unidirectional GRU of width d. Transport: key dimension 64, window w=7 over S=3 source frames
(147 candidates per query), learned identity bias initialised at β=4, and gate bias −3. Correction
head: LayerNorm, then a zero-initialised linear map to C, then a per-channel scale initialised at 1.
Parameters: 21.75M (SHIFTWM) and 21.70M (Direct, AR, AR-TF).

PLUG-IN HEADS FOR PUBLISHED WORLD MODELS
For a published backbone with prediction Pk, the head outputs ˆZk = (1 −gk)Pk + gkTk, where
Tk is the local 7×7 soft transport (with identity bias) of observed encoder tokens, and the gate
gk = σ(w⊤h + b) has w = 0, b = −4 at initialisation, so training starts numerically from the
unmodiﬁed backbone. Nothing else in the backbone, loss or schedule changes. V-JEPA 2-AC.
Queries come from the predictor’s normalised hidden state for each output block (1024-D), keys
and values from the layer-normalised ViT-g/16 tokens (16×16, 1408-D) of the last S=3 measured
frames, with a learned per-source key offset (0.16M parameters). The ﬁne-tuning objective is the
released one (teacher-forced L1 plus rollout L1). During rollouts the predictor consumes ˆZk as its
own prediction, while Tk only ever reads measured tokens. DINO-WM. We subclass the ofﬁcial
ViTPredictor (commit 0a9492f, otherwise unmodiﬁed): queries come from the predictor’s
output token, keys and values from its input token at the same slot, on the 14×14 DINOv2 patch
grid (S=1); proprioceptive and action channels are left untouched. The extra parameters are drawn
from a private random generator, so data order, initialisation and dropout of the base predictor are
identical in both arms.

C.3
BASELINES
Matched predictors.
All learned baselines share SHIFTWM’s memory encoder, decoder, width,
depth, optimiser and schedule, and differ only in the output head and rollout. This isolates the
contribution of transport from those of capacity and spatial communication. Direct has the same
cross-patch attention as SHIFTWM and differs only in lacking the transport and gate.
DINO-WM-style (AR-TF).
Following Zhou et al. (2025), the predictor regresses absolute next-
step patch features with teacher forcing and is rolled out autoregressively at test time. We use the
same frozen DINOv2 patches at native resolution.
External reference: V-JEPA 2-AC.

D.5
PLANNING RESULTS AND FAITHFULNESS OF THE HARNESS
Faithfulness check.
With the released LeWM PushT checkpoint and the ofﬁcial protocol, our
harness reaches 45/50, 44/50 and 43/50 successes for planner seeds 42/43/44 (mean 88%). An
adapter check conﬁrms that our pluggable predictor interface produces exactly the native LeWM
planning costs (maximum absolute difference 0). The LeWM paper reports 96 ± 2.8, averaged over
training seeds, whereas the released checkpoint is a single training seed. On TwoRoom and Reacher
the released checkpoints reach 43/41/44 and 42/44/41 successes (85.3% and 84.7%), against 87
and 86 reported by Maes et al. (2026b). Planning takes 0.41 s (PushT) and about 1.5 s (TwoRoom,
Reacher) per CEM plan on one H200.
D.6
ACTION SENSITIVITY AND EFFICIENCY
Accuracy versus action sensitivity.
A forecast that stays close to Z0 can score well on average
while barely responding to the commanded actions, and a planner then cannot rank candidate action
sequences. We measure this directly as the rate at which true future actions yield lower error than
actions from another episode (Figure 10, left). Anchored predictors can reach low open-loop error
while reacting weakly to actions; the action-contrastive objective moves SHIFTWM toward high
accuracy and high sensitivity. pending

E.2
SEGMENTATION VIEW OF THE FORECASTS
Does the moving arm land in the right place?
Feature error does not show where a forecast puts
the arm, so we read a segmentation out of every forecast (Figure 15). SAM 2.1 (Ravi et al., 2025)
tracks the robot arm (the instruments on Hamlyn) through the true future frames, and each forecast
patch is labelled arm or background by nearest-neighbour transfer from the observed frame alone
(protocol below). Placement is the distance between the centroid of the cells a forecast labels as arm
and the centroid of the true mask at t+k. On the half of the 1,332 held-out DROID windows where
the arm moves most, the mean placement error over k is 12.0 px for SHIFTWM, 12.2 for Direct, 13.9
for AR and 17.6 for persistence (320×180 frame): SHIFTWM removes +2.1 px relative to AR (95%
CI [+1.5, +2.7]), but only +0.6 px relative to Direct ([+0.0, +1.1]), a marginal difference; at k=10
SHIFTWM and Direct tie (16.7 vs. 16.8 px). On Hamlyn all learned models place the instruments
within a pixel of each other (SHIFTWM 12.3, Direct 12.4, AR 12.7 px; paired reductions +0.2 [+0.0,
+0.4] and +0.3 [+0.1, +0.6] px), all far ahead of persistence (16.6 px). Overlap tells the same story:
on moving DROID windows the arm read out of SHIFTWM has IoU +2.0 points above Direct ([+1.2,
+2.7]) and +4.2 above AR ([+3.3, +5.1]); on Hamlyn it ties Direct (+0.1, [-0.2, +0.4]; Table 18). The
examples in Figure 15a are selected for a large SHIFTWM placement advantage and illustrate what
that advantage looks like; the claims rest on the averages.

Protocol.
We ask whether the moving part of the scene lands where it should in each forecast,
without decoding pixels. Reference masks. On the observed frame t (the last history frame) of every
held-out window (test split; the planning suites have no test split, so their validation split is used;
stride 2 over all episodes, and an evenly spaced subset of 1,500 windows where there are more),
Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2024) detects a ﬁxed per-dataset text prompt (DROID, Bridge, RT-1:
“a robot arm”; Hamlyn: “a surgical instrument”, up to two non-overlapping boxes; Language-Table:
“a robot arm. a block.”, up to two boxes; IWS: the manipulated T block, box or rope). Its top box
prompts SAM 2.1 (Hiera-L) (Ravi et al., 2025), which segments frame t and tracks the mask through
the true future frames t+1, . . . , t+K with its video memory. Frames are segmented at their native
aspect ratio; each mask keeps its largest connected component and any component with ≥25% of
its area. For the rendered planning suites a text prompt is unreliable, so an exact colour threshold
of the agent/object is used instead. Quality control. A window is kept only if the detection score
is ≥0.3, the mask covers 3–128 of the 256 patches at t, the track is never lost and the mask area
never changes by more than 3× between consecutive frames; on DROID this keeps 1,332 of 1,500
windows (rejected: 104 without a conﬁdent detection, 45 with an arm too small or too large at t,
8 lost tracks, 11 area jumps), and on Hamlyn 1,017 of 1,500. Reading a mask out of a forecast.
Masks are area-averaged onto the 16×16 patch grid (a patch is foreground if ≥50% covered). Each
patch of a forecast ˆZk is labelled by nearest-neighbour transfer from the observed frame only: it is
foreground if its most similar (cosine) foreground patch of Z0 is more similar than its most similar
background patch. This labeller was ﬁxed in a DROID pilot as the one with the highest IoU when
applied to the true future features, a criterion that involves no forecast (on DROID at k=10: nearest-
neighbour 0.70, k-means prototypes 0.60, single prototypes 0.42). Metric. IoU between the forecast
mask and the tracked SAM mask of the true future frame, per horizon and averaged over k, on
all windows and on moving windows (overlap of the masks at t and t+K below the median). CIs
are 95% bootstrap intervals over episodes; gains are paired differences per episode. Placement.
The predicted centroid is the mean of the centres of the cells labelled arm; the true centroid is the
coverage-weighted mean of cell centres under the tracked SAM mask; distances are in pixels of
the displayed native-aspect frame (DROID 320×180, Hamlyn 396×224) and, in summary.json,
in patches. At a given k, windows in which any compared method labels no cell are dropped for
all methods (paired; this affects at most 1% of DROID and 3% of Hamlyn windows). Examples
show where SHIFTWM does best, by a ﬁxed rule: among QC-passing moving windows in which
SHIFTWM’s k=10 placement error is below its median and the labeller applied to the true future
features is at least median-accurate (a method-independent sanity check), the two (from distinct
episodes) with the largest k=10 placement advantage min(Direct, AR) −SHIFTWM. All methods
are always shown on the same window and the same frame. The examples are not representative;
averages and paired CIs are in Tables 18 and 19. Checkpoints are the ﬁnal ones used in the main
tables.

Results.
Table 19 lists placement error and Table 18 IoU per dataset and method (Figures 16
and 17 show both example windows per dataset). On DROID, SHIFTWM has the highest IoU
at every reported horizon, on all and on moving windows, with paired CIs that exclude zero
against both Direct and AR. On Hamlyn the instruments move less within ten steps and all
three learned predictors are within half an IoU point: SHIFTWM ties Direct and is marginally
ahead of AR; the segmentation view does not separate the methods there.
Datasets whose
ShiftWM/Direct checkpoints are not trained yet are marked pending and are ﬁlled in by re-running
scripts/v2/segments_all.sh.
Caveats.
(i) The readout measures where a forecast puts content that looks like the observed fore-
ground; it cannot credit content that was not visible at t. (ii) The labeller is imperfect: applied to the
true future features it reaches IoU 0.70 on DROID at k=10, so absolute values are lower bounds on
localisation quality; on Hamlyn several forecasts slightly exceed this reference, because smoother
forecasts can be labelled more easily than the true features. (iii) SAM 2.1 occasionally includes a
grasped object or tool-attached structure in the tracked mask; QC removes lost or exploding tracks
but not such merges. On Hamlyn most rejected windows (363 of 483) are close-ups in which the
instruments cover more than half of the frame. (iv) One seed per method.

QUALITATIVE RESULTS
Example selection.
Illustrations in the main paper use selected held-out windows, and every
method is always shown on the same window. The teaser strip, the bottom row of Figure 2 and
Figure 11 use the DROID test window (among the 30% with the most motion) with the largest
step-10 advantage of SHIFTWM over the better of Direct and AR; the win maps of Figure 6 use the
high-motion windows with the largest advantage over Direct. These show the mechanism where it
matters; typical behaviour is given by the aggregate results, and the galleries below use a random
selection. In Figure 1(b) every model’s own step-10 features (and the true ones) are decoded to RGB
by the feature-to-RGB decoder of Appendix D.3, shown at the camera’s aspect ratio and cropped
to the same arm region (top 12×11 patches); the corner numbers are feature errors of the whole
frame. In Figure 1(c), ∆error compares SHIFTWM with Direct (the strongest learned baseline) on
DROID and Hamlyn, and with the same published model without the head for V-JEPA 2-AC and
DINO-WM; skill is the share of persistence error removed.
