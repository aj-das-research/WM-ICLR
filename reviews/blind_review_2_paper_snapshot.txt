ABSTRACT
A world model forecasts how a scene responds to actions. Forecasting in the fea-
ture space of a frozen visual foundation model yields compact models that support
planning without generating pixels. Current latent world models such as DINO-
WM and V-JEPA 2-AC regenerate the entire patch-feature grid at every future step
and usually feed their own predictions back as inputs. Yet in manipulation and
surgical scenes most content persists and only moves. Regenerating it wastes ca-
pacity, blurs moving objects, corrupts static background and lets errors compound
over the rollout. We introduce SHIFTWM, a world model that forecasts by moving
what it has already seen. For each preﬁx of future actions it predicts a local soft
transport of measured patch features, a gate that decides where transport applies,
and a correction for content that transport cannot explain. Every horizon is de-
coded directly from observed features, and a simple motion model shows that the
MSE-optimal forecast is itself such a transport. We compare SHIFTWM with pre-
dictors that share its frozen encoder and architecture on held-out real robot video
(DROID, Language-Table) and seven surgical robot tasks, and attach it as a plug-
in head to two published world models. On held-out real robot video (DROID),
SHIFTWM lowers forecast error by 7.6% relative to a matched direct predictor
and by 12.2% relative to a matched recursive one; on block pushing (Language-
Table) it lowers error by 6.9% and 8.6%, and on surgical tasks the gains are smaller
(3.6% and 4.0%). Added to V-JEPA 2-AC under an identical ﬁne-tuning budget,
it lowers forecast error by 15.7%; added to DINO-WM, it lowers latent error by
8.0% on PushT but raises it by 18.4% on Wall.

INTRODUCTION
A world model predicts how observations change in response to actions, so that an agent can evaluate
candidate actions before executing them. A growing line of work forecasts in the patch-feature space
of a frozen self-supervised encoder such as DINOv2 or V-JEPA 2, which gives compact models that
plan without generating pixels (Zhou et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b; Sobal
et al., 2025). The quality of such a model is decided by how accurately it forecasts the future feature
grid, many steps ahead and on scenes it has not seen.
Current latent world models are generative in feature space: at every future step they regress the
complete grid of patch features, and most feed their own predictions back as inputs. In manipula-
tion and surgical video, however, most of the scene persists: the table, tissue, tools and the robot
move, but they are rarely created or destroyed. Regenerating persistent content causes three concrete
problems. Static background must be rewritten exactly, yet on held-out robot video every matched
regression predictor we train is worse than simply copying the last frame on static patches. Moving
content must be synthesised as a displacement in feature space, which blurs object boundaries. Re-
cursive rollouts compound these errors: once rolled out, a DINO-WM-style predictor trained with
teacher forcing is worse than persistence on held-out robot video. Yet much of the future is already
observed: on moving patches of held-out DROID, copying the single best observed feature from a
local window removes 51% of the error of staying put. Pixel-space video prediction and dense fea-
ture forecasting exploit such motion by warping (Finn et al., 2016; De Brabandere et al., 2016; Liu
et al., 2017; Šari´c et al., 2020), but not in action-conditioned latent world models used for planning.
We introduce SHIFTWM, a latent world model that forecasts by moving what it has already seen
(Figures 1 and 2). Instead of regressing the next feature grid, it predicts, for every future step, a
motion of the grids it has observed: each patch softly selects where its content comes from within a
local window of measured features, a gate decides whether it stays or moves, and a small correction
adds what motion cannot explain. The motion is conditioned on the causal preﬁx of future actions,
and every horizon is decoded in parallel from measured features, so predictions are never fed back.
The transport weights, gate and correction are explicit tensors that show where the model predicts
motion, and because the design is an output head, it can be attached to published world models.
Our evaluation suite has 17 benchmarks: ﬁve real-robot datasets (DROID, BridgeData V2, RT-1,
Language-Table, IWS), seven surgical robot tasks from Open-H, and ﬁve simulated planning suites
(Figure 4). This version reports held-out DROID, Language-Table, the seven surgical tasks, and
plug-ins to V-JEPA 2-AC and DINO-WM (PushT, Wall); the other benchmarks are marked pending.
All learned baselines share SHIFTWM’s frozen encoder, architecture and training budget and differ
only in how the forecast is formed. We score feature error on held-out episodes (held-out recording
sessions for DROID) with paired bootstrap intervals, and train the plug-ins under the published
models’ own protocols.
Contributions.
• We introduce SHIFTWM, a latent world model whose output is a learned soft local transport
of frozen foundation-model patch features, conditioned on the action preﬁx, gated against the
identity, corrected for new content and decoded in parallel for every horizon. It adds < 0.3%
parameters to a matched predictor (Section 3).
• We show that under a simple motion model the MSE-optimal forecast is a transport of observed
features, whereas an additive head must represent the feature contrast along the motion, and
we check the premise without a model: on moving DROID patches, copying the best observed
feature removes 51% of the error of staying put (Sections 3.5 and 5.3).
• We set up an evaluation suite of 17 benchmarks across real robots, surgical robotics and simu-
lated planning, and demonstrate against predictors with the same encoder and architecture that
SHIFTWM lowers held-out DROID error by 7.6% relative to a direct and 12.2% relative to a re-
cursive predictor, with small gains on seven surgical tasks (3.6%). It is the only learned predictor
below persistence on static DROID patches, and removing transport raises its moving-patch error
by 56.8% (Section 5).

RELATED WORK
Latent and foundation-feature world models.
A recent line of work predicts dynamics in the
embedding space of a pretrained or jointly learned encoder instead of in pixels. DINO-WM trains
a causal ViT predictor on frozen DINOv2 patch features and plans with model-predictive con-
trol (Zhou et al., 2025; Oquab et al., 2024); PLDM, LeWM and JEPA-WMs learn joint-embedding
predictive models from ofﬂine trajectories (Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b; Balestriero &
LeCun, 2025; Terver et al., 2026); and V-JEPA 2-AC post-trains an action-conditioned predictor on
DROID for zero-shot robot planning (Assran et al., 2025; Khazatsky et al., 2024). DINO-world,
DINO-Foresight, VFMF and FlowWM forecast future foundation features, deterministically or gen-
eratively (Baldassarre et al., 2025; Karypidis et al., 2025; Boduljak et al., 2025; Porcher et al., 2026);
DDP-WM separates sparse dynamic regions from a static background, RLA-WM models residual
latent actions (Yin et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a), and several analyses ask which latent spaces
and objectives make predictions action-relevant (Nilaksh et al., 2026; Yeom et al., 2026; Zhang et al.,
2026b; Huang et al., 2026). Pixel-space generative world models and model-based reinforcement
learning address rendering ﬁdelity and online control with their own encoders, data and training
budgets (Bar et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025a; Hafner et al., 2025; Hansen et al., 2024); we treat them
as complementary (Appendix A.5). In all latent models above, the predictor synthesises every fu-
ture feature map, including content that only moves. SHIFTWM keeps the frozen-encoder interface
but changes the output parameterisation: each forecast is a transport of observed features plus a
correction, and the same head can be attached to these models.
Recursive versus direct multi-step prediction.
Iterating a one-step model compounds its errors,
a classic observation in multi-step learning (Venkatraman et al., 2015). Most latent world models
are nevertheless trained for one step and rolled out recursively. DINO-WM uses teacher forcing
and autoregressive rollouts (Zhou et al., 2025), V-JEPA 2-AC adds a rollout loss to teacher-forced
training (Assran et al., 2025), and PlaNet introduced multi-step latent overshooting to reduce the
mismatch between one-step training and multi-step use (Hafner et al., 2019). Anchored navigation
world models resist drift by conditioning on anchor frames (Luan et al., 2026), and decision-oriented
objectives align latent predictions with the costs that a planner ranks (Wang et al., 2026a). Direct
prediction of every horizon avoids feedback, but a direct regressor must still regenerate the whole
grid, and anchoring to the present makes it easy to ignore actions. SHIFTWM, like our Direct
baseline, decodes every horizon from measured features; it differs in what it decodes, and it adds an
action-contrastive objective to keep anchored forecasts sensitive to the actions a planner compares.
Motion-, ﬂow- and feature-warping prediction.
Predicting the future by moving existing con-
tent has a long history. In pixel space, CDNA and DNA transform the previous frame with action-
conditioned kernels or motion distributions (Finn et al., 2016), dynamic ﬁlter networks generate
input-dependent ﬁlters (De Brabandere et al., 2016), Vondrick and Torralba transform past pixels
with adversarially trained transformers (Vondrick & Torralba, 2017), SNA adds skip connections
to the ﬁrst frame to handle occlusion (Ebert et al., 2017), and voxel ﬂow resamples frames along
predicted ﬂow (Liu et al., 2017); SAVP and MCVD add stochastic and diffusion models (Lee et al.,
2018; Voleti et al., 2022). In feature space, Deep Feature Flow propagates CNN features along opti-
cal ﬂow instead of recomputing them (Zhu et al., 2017), F2F forecasts future convolutional features
for semantic and instance segmentation (Luc et al., 2017; 2018), and F2M warps observed features
with a forecast feature-motion ﬁeld and blends them with a directly regressed forecast (Šari´c et al.,
2020). Recent robot world models reintroduce explicit motion: FlowDreamer predicts 3D scene
ﬂow before diffusion rendering (Guo et al., 2026a), AHEAD conditions token forecasts on optical-
ﬂow velocity (Syed et al., 2026), and object-centric models impose structure through slots (Wu et al.,
2023; Mosbach et al., 2025). Feature-warping forecasters are not action-conditioned and serve dense
recognition, while robot models warp pixels or ﬂow. SHIFTWM carries the warp-and-correct idea
into latent world models: a learned soft local transport of frozen foundation-model patch features,
conditioned on the action preﬁx, gated against the identity with a correction, decoded in parallel for
every horizon, and usable as a plug-in head for published world models.

METHOD
Method: SHIFTWM.
SHIFTWM is a latent world model whose output is a motion of the feature
grids it has observed. For every future step and every patch, it predicts where in a local window
of measured features the patch’s content comes from, whether the patch stays or moves, and a
correction for content that motion cannot explain (Figure 2; the lower row shows each term of
Equation (2) on a held-out DROID window).
3.1
PROBLEM SETTING
A frozen encoder E maps an image to a grid of N = G × G patch features Zt ∈RN×C. Given H
observed grids Z−H+1:0, the actions between them a−H+1:−1, and a future action sequence a0:K−1,
a world model predicts ˆZ1:K. The forecast ˆZk may depend only on a<k (causality). Features are
standardised per channel with train-split statistics that are shared across positions.
3.2
ARCHITECTURE
Memory encoder. Each observed patch feature is projected to width d and receives a spatial embed-
ding, a frame embedding and an embedding of the action applied after that frame. Le transformer
layers over all H ·N tokens give memory states M ∈RH×N×d. Action-preﬁx queries. A unidirec-
tional GRU over embedded actions gives a causal preﬁx state, to which we add a horizon embedding
to form ck. Query tokens start from the memory states of the last frame, and Ld decoder layers apply
self-attention across patches and cross-attention to M, each modulated by ck through adaptive layer
normalisation with zero-initialised gains (Peebles & Xie, 2023). The result is hk,i ∈Rd for patch i
at step k; all K steps are decoded in parallel.

Baselines.
All learned predictors share a frozen DINOv2-S/14 encoder (16×16 patches, no pool-
ing), the same memory encoder and decoder (width 384, Le=Ld=4, ≈22M parameters), optimiser,
schedule, steps and batch size (Appendix B.1); they differ only in the output head and rollout. Di-
rect replaces Equation (2) with Z0 + Wohk; it has the same cross-patch attention, so it controls for
spatial communication. AR predicts a residual to the most recent grid and is rolled out recursively,
with gradients through the rollout. AR-TF follows DINO-WM: it is trained with teacher forcing to
predict absolute features and is rolled out at test time. Persistence copies Z0, and Linear extrapo-
lates the last feature difference. As published models we use V-JEPA 2-AC and DINO-WM, with
and without the SHIFTWM head (Section 5.2).
Metrics and protocol.
The primary metric is standardised feature MSE on held-out episodes,
averaged over horizons and at the ﬁnal step. We also report skill, the fraction of persistence error
a model removes; being scale-free, it compares models in different feature spaces. Episodes are
the statistical unit (Section 5.3). For control we plug each predictor into the same model-predictive
planner (CEM) with the ofﬁcial budget of each benchmark; the cost is the feature distance between
the predicted and the goal grid.

Because SHIFTWM is an output head, we attach it to two published models without other changes:
it blends the backbone’s prediction P with transported observed features, ˆZ = (1−g)P+g T, with

dows, 24.6% after ﬁne-tuning, and 36.4% after ﬁne-tuning with the head under the identical budget

CONCLUSION
SHIFTWM forecasts by moving what it has already seen, plus a small correction. Against matched
predictors it has the lowest held-out error on real robot video and, by a small margin, on surgical
robot tasks; as a plug-in head it improves V-JEPA 2-AC and DINO-WM on PushT, and it makes
predicted motion explicit. The analyses trace the gain to transport on moving content and an exact
identity path on static content.
Limitations.
The plug-in does not help uniformly: on DINO-WM Wall, with a single history
frame, it raises latent error. Gains on the surgical tasks are small, and arm placement improves
only marginally over Direct. Newly revealed content depends on the correction, the forecast is
deterministic, pixel metrics rely on a separate decoder, and the surgical data are scene-camera views
of dVRK training tasks, not in-vivo endoscopy.

A
BACKGROUND AND PROBLEM SETTING
A.1
THE TASK AND WHY IT MATTERS
Task.
An action-conditioned latent world model receives the last H observed frames of a scene,
encoded by a frozen visual foundation model into grids of patch features, the actions applied between
them, and a candidate sequence of K future actions. It must predict the patch-feature grids of the
next K frames, without seeing them. It never generates pixels: the forecast lives in the same feature
space that downstream consumers read.
Why it is useful.
Such forecasts serve three uses. (i) Planning: a model-predictive planner sam-
ples many candidate action sequences, forecasts each, and executes the one whose forecast is closest
to a goal (Zhou et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b); forecast accuracy and sensitivity to the actions de-
cide which candidate wins. (ii) Control and policy evaluation: a world model can score a policy’s
proposed actions before they are executed on a real robot. (iii) Anticipation: in surgical robotics,
forecasting where instruments and tissue will be a few seconds ahead is a building block for assis-
tance and safety monitoring. All three need forecasts that are accurate many steps ahead, respond to
the actions, and hold on scenes not seen in training.

An example.
Figure 7 shows the task on two held-out episodes, one from DROID (a Franka arm
in a real kitchen) and one from the Open-H Hamlyn surgical tasks (a dVRK instrument over a
training phantom). The world model receives the last H=3 frames t−2, t−1, t, encoded by a frozen
DINOv2 encoder into 16×16 grids of 384-D patch features, the actions applied between them, and
a sequence of K=10 future action blocks (the commanded end-effector trajectory, bottom rows). It
must predict the feature grids Zt+1, . . . , Zt+10, 3.3 s into the future, without seeing any future frame.
The episodes are chosen by a ﬁxed rule (largest feature change among test episodes; Appendix E.3).
The rightmost panels show where the features actually change between t and t+10. Change is
concentrated on the robot and on the objects it touches: in these two windows a quarter of the
patches carry more than 40% of the total change, while large parts of the scene change little. This is
the structure that SHIFTWM exploits: most future patch features are already present in the observed
grid, displaced by motion, and only a small part is new content.
A.2
WHY EXISTING WORLD MODELS FALL SHORT
Current latent world models regress the complete feature grid at every future step, and most roll out
recursively (Section 2). Our matched baselines isolate these two design choices (Section 4), and the
held-out results show three concrete failure modes.
1. Regenerating static content corrupts it. On static DROID patches, every learned regression
predictor (Direct, AR, AR-TF) has higher error than simply copying the last observed frame
(Table 17). A regression head must output exactly zero change there, and any approximation
error damages background that should not change.
2. Regenerating moving content blurs it. On moving patches, an additive head must write out
the feature contrast along the motion (Proposition 2). At k=10, Direct and AR retain less of the
true spatial contrast than SHIFTWM (Figure 11b), and SHIFTWM has 7.2% and 10.0% lower
moving-patch error than Direct and AR (Table 17).
3. Recursion compounds error. Predictors that consume their own forecasts accumulate error
with the horizon (Proposition 3, Figure 5). The DINO-WM-style AR-TF, trained with teacher
forcing, is worse than persistence on DROID once rolled out, and SHIFTWM has 12.2% lower
error than the rollout-trained AR (Table 1).
At the same time, much of the future is already observed: on moving DROID patches, copying the
single best observed feature from the transport window removes 51% of the error of staying put
(Figure 12b). SHIFTWM is designed around this structure: it keeps static content through an exact
identity path, moves observed content through transport, and decodes every horizon from measured
features.

A.5
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
Generative world models and model-based reinforcement learning.
Pixel-space generative
world models learn high-ﬁdelity, action-controllable video simulators. NWM conditions a diffusion
transformer on navigation actions (Bar et al., 2025); Cosmos and Cosmos-Predict2.5 provide large
video foundation models for physical AI (NVIDIA, 2025a;b) that Cosmos Policy adapts for con-
trol (Kim et al., 2026); Ctrl-World, IRASim, UniSim and IWS target robot manipulation and policy
evaluation (Guo et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026b); and the Genie
family generates interactive environments from video, image or text prompts (Bruce et al., 2024;
Parker-Holder & Fruchter, 2025). Model-based reinforcement learning instead learns task-speciﬁc
latent dynamics jointly with rewards and policies, from PlaNet and DreamerV3 to TD-MPC2, IRIS
and DIAMOND (Hafner et al., 2019; 2025; Hansen et al., 2024; Micheli et al., 2023; Alonso et al.,
2024). These systems optimise sample ﬁdelity or online return with their own encoders and data,
so they do not share our contract of a ﬁxed frozen encoder, identical observations and actions, and
targets in common feature coordinates. We therefore treat them as complementary rather than as
compute-matched baselines.
World models for surgery and evaluation suites.
Surgical world modelling is driven by gen-
erative video models: Endora simulates endoscopy video (Li et al., 2024), SurgWM learns latent-
action-controllable surgical video (Koju et al., 2025), SurgVista targets long-horizon instrument–
tissue dynamics (Pan et al., 2026), SWoMo combines neural and symbolic components for cataract
surgery (Sivakumar et al., 2026), and Cosmos-H-Surgical learns surgical robot policies from videos
through world modelling (He et al., 2025). Open-H-Embodiment releases paired video and kinemat-
ics across surgical platforms, together with a kinematics-conditioned surgical simulator (Open-H-
Embodiment Consortium, 2026); SutureBot, JIGSAWS, Cholec80 and CholecT50 provide surgical
demonstrations and task, skill and workﬂow annotations (Haworth et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2014;
Twinanda et al., 2017; Nwoye et al., 2022), and SurgWMBench benchmarks instrument-motion
planning (Liu et al., 2026). We use Open-H dVRK recordings to test kinematics-conditioned fore-
casting in feature space, a regime these pixel generators do not target. Robot datasets such as
DROID, BridgeData V2 and Open X-Embodiment supply action-labelled video (Khazatsky et al.,
2024; Walke et al., 2023; Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024). WorldScore, WorldModel-
Bench, EWMBench and Physics-IQ score generated video for controllability and physical plausi-
bility (Duan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025; Motamed et al., 2026), whereas stable-
worldmodel standardises latent planning evaluation (Maes et al., 2026a). We follow the latter for
control and report feature-space forecasting error for prediction.
Encoders.
SHIFTWM applies to any frozen patch encoder, e.g. DINOv2 or DINOv3 (Oquab et al.,
2024; Siméoni et al., 2025).
B
EXTENDED METHOD
B.1
ARCHITECTURE
Architecture.
Width d=384, 6 heads, MLP ratio 4, memory encoder Le=4 layers of full self-
attention over the H · N=768 tokens of H=3 observed 16×16 grids. Query decoder: Ld=4 layers,
each with self-attention over the N=256 query tokens, cross-attention to memory and an MLP,
modulated by AdaLN-zero on ck. Action embedding: a 2-layer MLP followed by a single-layer
unidirectional GRU of width d. Transport: key dimension 64, window w=7 over S=3 source frames
(147 candidates per query), learned identity bias initialised at β=4, and gate bias −3. Correction
head: LayerNorm, then a zero-initialised linear map to C, then a per-channel scale initialised at 1.
Parameters: 21.75M (SHIFTWM) and 21.70M (Direct, AR, AR-TF).

B.3
PLUG-IN HEADS FOR PUBLISHED WORLD MODELS
For a published backbone with prediction Pk, the head outputs ˆZk = (1 −gk)Pk + gkTk, where
Tk is the local 7×7 soft transport (with identity bias) of observed encoder tokens, and the gate
gk = σ(w⊤h + b) has w = 0, b = −4 at initialisation, so training starts numerically from the
unmodiﬁed backbone. Nothing else in the backbone, loss or schedule changes. V-JEPA 2-AC.
Queries come from the predictor’s normalised hidden state for each output block (1024-D), keys
and values from the layer-normalised ViT-g/16 tokens (16×16, 1408-D) of the last S=3 measured
frames, with a learned per-source key offset (0.16M parameters). The ﬁne-tuning objective is the
released one (teacher-forced L1 plus rollout L1). During rollouts the predictor consumes ˆZk as its
own prediction, while Tk only ever reads measured tokens. DINO-WM. We subclass the ofﬁcial
ViTPredictor (commit 0a9492f, otherwise unmodiﬁed): queries come from the predictor’s
output token, keys and values from its input token at the same slot, on the 14×14 DINOv2 patch
grid (S=1); proprioceptive and action channels are left untouched. The extra parameters are drawn
from a private random generator, so data order, initialisation and dropout of the base predictor are
identical in both arms. Planning uses the ofﬁcial DINO-WM planner at a reduced budget that is
identical for both arms.

C.3
BASELINES
Matched predictors.
All learned baselines share SHIFTWM’s memory encoder, decoder, width,
depth, optimiser and schedule, and differ only in the output head and rollout. This isolates the
contribution of transport from those of capacity and spatial communication. Direct has the same
cross-patch attention as SHIFTWM and differs only in lacking the transport and gate.
DINO-WM-style (AR-TF).
Following Zhou et al. (2025), the predictor regresses absolute next-
step patch features with teacher forcing and is rolled out autoregressively at test time. We use the
same frozen DINOv2 patches at native resolution.

Protocol.
We ask whether the moving part of the scene lands where it should in each forecast,
without decoding pixels. Reference masks. On the observed frame t (the last history frame) of every
held-out window (test split; the planning suites have no test split, so their validation split is used;
stride 2 over all episodes, and an evenly spaced subset of 1,500 windows where there are more),
Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2024) detects a ﬁxed per-dataset text prompt (DROID, Bridge, RT-1:
“a robot arm”; Hamlyn: “a surgical instrument”, up to two non-overlapping boxes; Language-Table:
“a robot arm. a block.”, up to two boxes; IWS: the manipulated T block, box or rope). Its top box
prompts SAM 2.1 (Hiera-L) (Ravi et al., 2025), which segments frame t and tracks the mask through
the true future frames t+1, . . . , t+K with its video memory. Frames are segmented at their native
aspect ratio; each mask keeps its largest connected component and any component with ≥25% of
its area. For the rendered planning suites a text prompt is unreliable, so an exact colour threshold
of the agent/object is used instead. Quality control. A window is kept only if the detection score
is ≥0.3, the mask covers 3–128 of the 256 patches at t, the track is never lost and the mask area
never changes by more than 3× between consecutive frames; on DROID this keeps 1,332 of 1,500
windows (rejected: 104 without a conﬁdent detection, 45 with an arm too small or too large at t,
8 lost tracks, 11 area jumps), and on Hamlyn 1,017 of 1,500. Reading a mask out of a forecast.
Masks are area-averaged onto the 16×16 patch grid (a patch is foreground if ≥50% covered). Each
patch of a forecast ˆZk is labelled by nearest-neighbour transfer from the observed frame only: it is
foreground if its most similar (cosine) foreground patch of Z0 is more similar than its most similar
background patch. This labeller was ﬁxed in a DROID pilot as the one with the highest IoU when
applied to the true future features, a criterion that involves no forecast (on DROID at k=10: nearest-
neighbour 0.70, k-means prototypes 0.60, single prototypes 0.42). Metric. IoU between the forecast

E.4
DISCUSSION
When transport helps.
The gains are largest where content moves within the transport window
and keeps its appearance. On DROID, SHIFTWM has 7.2% lower moving-patch error than Direct,
and its advantage holds in every decile of motion (+5.1% to +13.2%). On the surgical tasks the
instruments move less within ten steps, the gains are small (3.6% vs. Direct), and the segmentation
view does not separate the learned models (Appendix E.2).
When it does not.
As a plug-in to DINO-WM on Wall, where the backbone conditions on a
single history frame, the head raises latent error by 18.4%. We have not yet tested the single-frame
explanation; the S=1 ablation in Table 3 addresses it. Content that is not visible in any observed
frame can only come from the correction (Figure 21), and motion larger than the window, such as
camera ego-motion, lies outside the transport prior; we removed the rare RT-1 episodes in which the
base moves.
Gate versus transport.
The gate knockout isolates transport on moving patches (56.8% higher
error without it, against 1.8% on static patches). On static patches, part of the advantage may come
from the gated identity path alone; separating the two requires a gated regression head without
transport, which we list as future work.
Uncertainty.
Under uncertain motion, the MSE-optimal forecast averages over plausible sources
(Proposition 1). SHIFTWM averages measured features rather than regressing a mean, but it remains
a deterministic model; stochastic feature forecasters (Boduljak et al., 2025; Porcher et al., 2026)
target multimodal futures and are complementary.
